Joel M. Dalrymple Memorial Award

Recipient

Rohit Jangra

Louisiana State University (LSU) Health Shreveport, Shreveport, Louisiana, USA.

Award Lecture
Unlocking hantavirus entry: from discovery to scalable genetics.
Biography

Rohit K. Jangra is an Assistant Professor at LSU Health Shreveport investigating hantavirus entry. Trained as a veterinarian in India, he began research studying foot-and-mouth disease virus and earned his PhD at the University of Texas Medical Branch, where he studied hepatitis C virus replication. His work combines discovery with experimental innovation, contributing to the identification of protocadherin-1 as a hantavirus receptor and the development of surrogate-virus systems for scalable genetic analysis. His laboratory uses these tools to connect glycoprotein function with receptor recognition and protective immunity. He is a recipient of the 2026 Joel M. Dalrymple Memorial Award.

Ho Wang Lee Lifetime Award

Co-recipients

Gregory Mertz

Center for Global Health, Department of Internal Medicine, University of New Mexico

Award Lecture
A 33-Year Journey with Hantavirus Cardiopulmonary Syndrome.
Biography

Dr Greg Mertz, FACP, FIDSA, a graduate of Johns Hopkins University and Rush Medical College, completed internal medicine residency and infectious diseases fellowship training at the University of Washington. He is Emeritus Professor of Internal Medicine at the University of New Mexico, where he was ID Division Chief for 17 years. He has studied hantavirus cardiopulmonary syndrome in the US and Chile for over 30 years. He was a CDC consultant, invited WHO clinical management workgroup member and WHO webinar speaker during the 2026 Hondius Andes virus outbreak and is co-editor for hantavirus infections for UpToDate.

Award lecture abstract

I am deeply honored be co-recipient of the Ho Wang Lee Lifetime Award 2026 with Dr. Pablo Vial, my colleague and friend for the past three decades. When hantavirus cardiopulmonary syndrome was first recognized in the U.S. Southwest in 1993, I was the ID chief at the University of New Mexico Hospital. My background was in clinical virology, having done research on rabies virus, HSV, VZV, CMV and HIV, and I was a member of the NIAID Collaborative Antiviral Study Group (CASG).

In 1993 UNM served as the primary hantavirus treatment center for the region, and we responded to calls from physicians throughout the U.S. In 1994, the CASG asked me to design and lead a multicenter, placebo-controlled trial of IV ribavirin for HCPS. Ribavirin appeared ineffective when initiated in the cardiopulmonary phase, but we gained insights that informed patient care and subsequent studies.

When HCPS secondary to Andes virus was identified in Argentina and Chile, Pablo and I led a group that successfully applied to study hantavirus ecology, natural history of human infection, transmission and disease management in Chile. We received NIH/NIAID research and training grant support from 1999-2017, but we are equally proud that collaborative research has continued in Chile with local support.

Dr. Marcela Ferrés, a senior member of our group, led a large NIAID-funded prospective study of household contacts of index patients with HCPS in Chile that identified risk factors for person-to-person transmission in households and was also the first to report detection of viral RNA in blood for up to 15 days prior to the onset of prodromal symptoms or antibody responses. This and other studies conducted by our group informed our understanding of person-to-person transmission of Andes virus and helped predict the course of the 2026 MV Hondius outbreak. Finally, while the international response to the Hondius outbreak was an example of international cooperation, it was also characterized by infection control overreach that resulted in inappropriate confinement and risk of medical harm.
